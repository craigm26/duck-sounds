What it is

The Microduck is a small two-legged robot from Pollen Robotics — the lab behind the Reachy arms. It is about the height of a wine bottle, walks on two feet, looks around, picks things up in its beak, and quacks.

It is not a toy with a motor in it. It is a research robot that happens to be charming, and everything about how it moves is learned rather than scripted.

How it walks

Most robots walk by being told exactly where to put each foot. This one was not told. It practised in simulation until it worked out walking for itself, and what came out of that practice is a small neural network — about two hundred thousand numbers.

Fifty times a second the robot hands that network 61 numbers describing how it is currently doing: where its joints are, how fast they are moving, which way is down, and where you have asked it to go. The network answers with 14 joint positions. That answer is the readout at the top of this page.

Those 61 numbers include which way is down and how fast the body is turning — and that is the part that matters. Walking is a conversation with the floor: the network is listening for the moment a foot takes weight. Take the floor away and there is nothing to listen to, which is why running this network without physics does not produce a walk. It needs the ground as much as the duck does.

One integer

Every duck has its own voice

There is no sound file anywhere in the duck. Its quack is computed, from a single number that also decides how high it speaks, how nasal it is, how much of a buzz sits inside the quack, and how briskly it gets through a sentence.

That number comes from the robot's own serial. So two Microducks on the same table do not sound like one duck played twice — they sound like two animals, permanently, and yours will always sound like yours.

Open source

You can run the real thing

The duck's brain also exists as a Swift package. It reads the actual trained network and runs the actual walking policy, with no robot in the room — which is how the numbers on this page were produced, on a Raspberry Pi.
